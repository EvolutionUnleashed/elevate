# Slides and decks

Slide words follow writing.md and headlines-and-names.md. Charts and diagrams follow documents.md.

## What a presentation designer notices first

They read the titles alone, in order. In a deck that's sent and read, the titles should make the whole argument by themselves; if they say "Market", "Product", "Team", the deck is a table of contents. Then they look for the slide with nothing to say and the slide trying to say four things. And they ask whether each slide would still make sense if someone screenshotted it and shared it alone, because most slides end up that way.

## Talk deck or read deck

A talk deck is presented. The speaker carries the words, so a slide holds one idea: a few words, or a single picture or number. If the audience can read the talk off the screen, they will, and they'll stop listening. Words you cut from a talk slide go into the speaker notes.

A read deck is sent and read alone. Each title states its point as a full sentence ("Churn halves when onboarding includes a call"), one chart or diagram proves it, and a line of context finishes it. The headline length rules don't apply to these titles.

Most weak decks are a confused mix of the two: a document chopped into slides that nobody could present and nobody wants to read.

## The ladder

1. The default: topic titles over bullets, the same layout on every slide.
2. The default restyled: the same bullets on a nicer template, with icons.
3. An argument: titles that make the case, one idea per slide, and one slide the room leans in for.

## One idea per slide

The idea is in the title, the visual proves it, and nothing else competes. Five bullets that each need saying are five slides. Five that all say one thing are one line. If a slide needs a paragraph to explain its diagram, the diagram is wrong, so simplify the picture or split the slide.

## The arc

A deck needs a turn: a point where the room's view of the subject changes. Build to one slide the room leans in for, whether that's the number, the before and after, or the demo, and let everything before it set that up. Don't force every deck into the same set of beats. Find the turn this material actually has.

A device that runs through the talk, such as a card on every seat or a question you return to, gives it a spine, but it frames the content without replacing it. The room still needs concrete things to see and copy, such as a worked example or a before and after from someone like them. Fill the slot you were given; a ten-minute talk that runs five feels thin however clever its frame.

End on the strongest implication or the ask. A Q&A slide can follow, but "Thank you" and "Questions?" are never the deck's last word. Drop the agenda slide unless the audience has to find their way around a long session.

## Visual

Hold one grid and one type scale across the deck, and let the key slide of each section break it on purpose. Size type for the room or screen it will be read on, and check the deck at the size of a shared screen on a video call, because that's where many decks are seen. Every number on a slide gets a small source line, since the slide will travel without the speaker. Explain a term before a diagram uses it; a diagram that grows build by build beats a summary map shown up front. Pictures are real photos or purpose-made art, as images.md describes.

## The second ditch

- Three words on a black slide in a deck that will be read alone, so nobody can follow it without the speaker.
- A keynote-style deck of giant words for a meeting where people need the detail.
- Forty slides where twelve would do, because every bullet became a slide of its own.

## The tells

- Topic labels as titles ("Market Overview", "Our Solution").
- Every slide the same title over the same bullets.
- A row of icon, heading and sentence, three across ("Problem / Solution / Impact").
- Abstract gradient or blob backgrounds.
- A rounded generated picture on the right of every slide.
- A "Key Takeaways" slide that repeats the deck.
- Walls of small text, or emoji as bullets.
- Stock photography of handshakes, lightbulbs and mountaintops.
- Slide numbers dressed up as design ("01 / 12").

## Before you hand it over

Look at the whole deck as a sheet of thumbnails. You should be able to see the turn and the slide that matters from across the room. If every thumbnail looks the same, so will the talk.
