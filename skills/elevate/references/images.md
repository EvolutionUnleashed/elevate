# Images, illustrations, logos, thumbnails and video

For pictures you make or prompt for: hero art, post images, illustrations, logos, social graphics, thumbnails, and short films or animation.

## What an art director notices first

Whether the picture is the first metaphor anyone would reach for. Whether it agrees with the sentence beside it. Where the energy is, and whether anyone chose it. Then the finish at full size: hands, teeth, text, the faces in the background, the plastic sheen on skin, the purple glow that says a model made this.

## The ladder

1. The stock metaphor: a lightbulb for an idea, a rocket for a launch.
2. The stock metaphor beautifully rendered, which is where most "better" prompts stop.
3. A picture built on the idea itself, committed to all the way.

## Commit to the idea

Play the idea for real. A picture that names its idea and then plays it small is boring. If the idea is "one tool replaces the clutter", a tidy desk plays it small. A shelf with dust outlines where nine old tools used to stand, and one phone in the middle, plays it for real, and the viewer does the counting.

Give it the energy the subject calls for, and decide where that energy lives: a gesture caught mid-action, an expression that's doing something, a crop that implies more outside the frame. A character smiling blankly into the lens under bright light is the model's default, and so is muted, static and half-hidden. A calm, serious or sad subject commits to that mood instead. Muted is only dull when nobody chose it.

Keep the hero prop fully in view, and make props belong to the subject's world. A stock prop could belong to any brand, so shape it, colour it or badge it until it could only be this one, without slapping words on it.

Make the picture agree with the argument. If the message is "one assistant that knows you", a crowd of robots says the opposite. Check every picture against the sentence it sits beside.

Give each image one idea. In a set, hold one look across all of them and never repeat a composition.

## The second ditch

- The tasteful still life: no people, a coffee cup or a face-down phone, soft morning window light, a bare wall left for the title, "calm, unhurried". It's what most "less AI" prompts converge on, so it reads as stock.
- Fake film grain and "shot on Portra" on everything, worn to look less AI.
- Wobbly hand-drawn styles chosen for the same reason.
- Quirky or surreal for its own sake, with no idea underneath.

## The tells

- The AI look: glowing blue and purple circuitry, a robot hand reaching for a human hand, a brain made of wires, holographic dashboards, a generic futuristic city.
- Stock metaphors: lightbulbs, rockets, handshakes, puzzle pieces, gears, dartboards, mountain summits.
- The finish: plastic skin, waxy faces, oversaturated HDR, lens flare, everything at golden hour, a centred subject in a gradient void.
- Garbled text inside the image.
- Stock tableaus: a team high-fiving, a person pointing at a laptop.
- Cinematic drama around an ordinary subject.

## Writing image prompts

Concrete beats adjectives. Name the subject, the action, the setting, the light, the lens or medium, and the palette. "A postwoman mid-stride up a steep wet street, bag swinging out behind her, lit only by the shop window beside her, shot low on a 28mm lens, palette held to slate, rust and off-white" beats "a stunning, vibrant, high-quality image".

Name one medium and its limits, such as "flat gouache, visible brush texture, three colours, no gradients". Leaving it blank gets you the model's default, which is the AI look, and stacking four styles gets you mush.

Put exclusions in the negative field if the tool has one. In a single-box prompt, listing what you don't want can summon it, so describe what is there instead: an empty street rather than "no cars".

Set the aspect ratio for where it will live, such as 16:9 for a post header or 4:5 for a feed.

Use references when you have them, such as a character sheet or a product photo, and describe only what changes.

Set the lettering yourself when it must be in the brand's typeface or exactly right. Several current models render short text well, but none of them will match a brand.

Generate several when it's free or they've asked, and ask first if it costs credits. Then edit the best one rather than rerolling, and inspect it at full size before it goes anywhere.

## Logos

A logo has to work at sixteen pixels and on the side of a building. Test it in one colour, small, before anything else. Give it one idea, no gradients or 3D, and nothing that only works with an effect behind it. If the brand already has a logo, use the real file and never redraw or decorate it.

## Thumbnails and social graphics

One focal point, readable at the size it will actually be seen, so shrink it and look again. A face or character with a clear expression usually beats a wide scene, though the shocked open mouth has become stock. Use two to four words at most, set big, and make them add to the title rather than repeat it. The image has to deliver what it promises, or the next click never comes.

## Video and animation

A film is beats with a turn rather than a slideshow with slow zooms, so something has to happen and something has to change. The first two seconds earn the rest: open on movement, a face or a surprise, never a logo sting. Caption the speech, because most feeds start muted, but cut on-screen text that only explains what the picture already shows. In vertical video, keep text out of the bottom fifth and the right edge, where the app's buttons sit. Keep a character on-model from shot to shot, plan the sound with the picture, and give each film one idea.
