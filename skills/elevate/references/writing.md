# Writing

For copy, posts, articles, newsletters, emails and scripts. Headlines, names and subject lines have their own file. Fiction, poetry, lyrics and translation have their own craft and aren't covered here.

## What a great editor notices first

Someone who has cut a lot of AI drafts reads for a few things before anything else. They look for the sentence where the piece starts explaining itself, the opener that clears its throat, and the benefit bolted onto the end of a sentence. They listen for the line nobody would ever say out loud, and they find the spot where the writer stopped trusting the reader. They want the reader to finish the thought, and they'd rather run one true line than a clever paragraph.

## Describing

AI describes. Whatever you point it at, it explains the features, then the benefits, then why the benefits matter. To a model, explaining completely is the most helpful thing it can do, and in an answer to a question that's right. In anything meant to move a reader, it leaves them nothing to do but be told.

"Sell the outcome, not the feature" doesn't fix this, because it only moves the describing from the product to the outcome:

1. Describing the thing: "Our wood-fired pizzas combine fresh ingredients with hand-stretched dough for an authentic Italian experience."
2. Describing the outcome: "Spend less time cooking and more time enjoying delicious food with the people you love."
3. Leaving room: "You've cooked all week. We've got tonight."

The third line never says you're tired or that you've earned a night off. The reader recognises the tiredness, then the relief, and supplies their own reasons. Because they filled it in, it feels written about them.

A line like that only works if nearly everyone in the audience has lived it. If it fits only some of them, it's an assumption about their lives, and the rest stop reading.

### Find this piece's own shape

The level is the lesson, not the shape. A couplet with a turn, used every time, becomes a template, and by the third piece readers can smell it. Watch for any opener you catch yourself reaching for twice. Other shapes leave room just as well:

- A first-person admission: "The week I started answering email once a day, the only person who noticed was me."
- A plain fact about the past that points at the future: "Offices used to employ someone whose whole job was remembering where things were filed."
- A general truth with the conclusion left out: "An email answered in four minutes teaches the sender to expect four minutes."

A thirty-word sentence can leave room as well as a five-word one. Room is about what you leave out, not how short you are.

### The second ditch in writing

Telling a model to stop describing usually produces one of these instead, and each is as bad as the description it replaced.

- The fact sheet: "Wood-fired pizza. Come in and have one." The explaining is gone and so is the reason to care. Room needs something for the reader to fill in from.
- The riddle: "Fire. Dough. Ten minutes." Compressed until it needs decoding. If a cold reader can't get it on one read, it fails.
- The invented scene: "It's 11:58pm on October 31st. You, a shoebox and a stapler." Made-up days, times and props are a model's idea of being specific, and readers take them as filler. Use something people recognise instead of a scene you made up.
- The outcome sneaking back: the room line, then "...so tax time is just another day." Stop on the offer.
- Chopped reasoning: "You can take on more work. But only if you hire. And hiring eats the profit." One thought cut into three announcements. Easy reading is a sentence that carries you through the reasoning, and fragments make you supply the joins yourself.

### Where room doesn't belong

Instructions, how-tos, technical documentation, reference material, support replies, legal text and prompts for AI models. There, explaining is the job: complete, literal and in order, with no step skipped. Raise those by making them clearer and better ordered, never by leaving gaps. See documents.md for guides.

## Long-form

Open on the most interesting true thing you have: a claim, a number, a real moment, an admission. Cut throat-clearing, rhetorical questions, "Imagine..." and any scene you made up, and don't open two pieces in a row the same way.

Build on a pattern the reader has already lived through, then let them see the next step themselves. A familiar pattern carries an unfamiliar idea further than any explanation of it.

Give each thought its own beat. The load-bearing idea gets room to land, and minor points stay minor.

Connect the reasoning. Let "because", "so" and "which" carry the logic, and make each paragraph hand on to the next. Cut the explaining, never the reasoning. A paragraph at the right level reads like this: "A restaurant that prints its menu once a season, because printing costs money, keeps a sold-out dish on it until spring, so the staff spend every night apologising for it. A menu on a screen changes the moment the kitchen runs out."

Introduce anything the reader doesn't know, in plain words, before you lean on it. A name they've never heard stops them cold.

Credit what the alternative does well before you show where it stops. Flattening a tool or a rival view to make a contrast easier tells every informed reader you don't know the field.

Write with calm authority. State the view and move on. Don't announce that something matters, don't justify your framing, and don't ask the reader to grade the thinking. Keep uncertainty where it's real, and give a number its source in whatever way the medium normally does.

Let a strong line end on its own full stop, without a consequence or a reassurance bolted on.

End on the strongest implication. A summary, a rhetorical question or a request for comments lets the energy out.

Use the real material. A real moment, number or mistake from the person's own work carries more than any anecdote you could invent. If you don't have one, say the idea plainly rather than fabricate it.

Use one example and let it plainly be one example. Never the roll call ("whether you're a coach, a consultant or a creator"). If an example would only fit some readers, say the idea at the level everyone shares.

## Emails

Write as one person to another, with the point early, in warm, flowing sentences. A single ask sits in the prose with its reason. Several questions someone has to answer one by one get numbered, so they can reply "1 yes, 2 not yet". Skip "I hope this finds you well" and "just circling back", and don't announce what you'll do in future emails. Do it.

## Scripts and anything spoken

Write for the mouth. Read it aloud at speaking pace, with contractions, plain words and sentences that fit in a breath. Keep production labels ("Beat 1", "Hook") out of what the speaker reads, and make the first line earn the second rather than opening on "Hey guys, in this video...".

## Voice

If you have the person's own writing, match it: their words, their rhythm, their rough edges. Natural repetition beats an unnatural synonym, so don't sand away a contraction, a long running sentence, or a swear word if it's theirs. A line they dictated as copy stays word for word. A ramble meant to inspire you is different, so take the facts and the feeling and write the piece fresh.

## The tells

These are tells in text a model wrote. Sweep for them after the level is fixed, never instead of fixing it, because deleting tells from a describing draft only gives you a tidier describing draft. Any of them can be right on purpose, and a technical term is never filler.

- Em dashes used for pauses or drama. Don't swap in colons; rewrite the sentence.
- Sets of three built for rhythm: "faster, smarter and more efficient".
- "It's not X, it's Y" as the default structure. Once in a piece at most.
- Several sentences in a row opening the same way.
- Fragment runs: "One place. One price. Zero stress."
- The same shape every time: a couplet with a turn, or an opener starting "Nobody..." or "Most people...".
- Balanced pairs and punchy closers: "People do your books. AI does the matching." "It drafts. You decide."
- A benefit bolted onto sentence after sentence: ", which means...", ", so you can...", ", and that's the point."
- Announced significance: "And that changes everything." "Here's why that matters." "Let that sink in."
- Narrating the writing: "Simply put", "Let me be clear", "Here's the truth".
- Stock openers: "In today's fast-paced world", "Imagine if", "What if I told you", "Let's talk about", "Here's the thing".
- Stock phrases: "what good looks like", "move the needle", "the secret sauce", "at the end of the day".
- Made-up scenes with weekdays and times: "It's 11pm on a Tuesday and you're still..."
- Filler words: delve, unlock, unleash, elevate, empower, harness, seamless, cutting-edge, game-changer, revolutionary, journey, tapestry, embark, foster, supercharge, synergy.
- Hedge stacks: "can potentially help you".
- Conditional truths squeezed into absolutes: "You'll never lose a lead again."
- Telling the reader what they feel: "You're exhausted." "You deserve better."
- Emoji bullets, bold sprinkled for emphasis, a header every three sentences.
- Every bullet opening with a bold label and a colon.
- Headings that name the method instead of the subject: "Breaking It Down", "The Bottom Line".
- Ending on a question to the reader or a request for comments, likes or shares.
