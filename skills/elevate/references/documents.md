# Documents, reports, one-pagers, proposals, plans and guides

For anything someone reads to decide or to act. The sentences follow writing.md, with one difference: a document someone has to act on leaves nothing out.

## What a strategy partner notices first

Whether the answer is findable in the first ten seconds, and whether the headings alone tell the story. Whether each recommendation has an owner and a date. Whether the diagram uses words the reader already knows. And whether it survives being opened on a phone, because most documents are.

## The ladder

1. The default: everything the writer knows, in the order they learned it, as bullets.
2. The default tidied: the same, with better headings and a summary that restates the title.
3. A document that answers: the decision in the first line, the reasoning in order, and the detail for whoever wants it.

## Lead with the answer

Open with the answer, decision or number in one sentence, then the reasoning, then the detail. Savings, pricing and performance documents lead with the figures, then say what produces them and how confident the estimate is.

Separate what's known from what's guessed. Label confirmed facts, sourced evidence, estimates and assumptions so nobody mistakes one for another.

Give every recommendation an owner and a date, and keep each decision or ask separate, with its reason beside it.

## The drawing

For plans, strategies and structures, put one clean drawing of the actual thing straight after the opening sentence, in words the reader already uses. If it's a ladder, draw a ladder, bottom rung to top. Use names and figures only, with no paragraphs inside the boxes, and put everything else underneath. If the plan fits in a paragraph, skip the drawing.

Every term in a diagram should be one the reader already knows or has just read. A map of words they haven't met is noise, and they'll stop there.

One diagram can carry the main argument. A picture of a trade-off makes it obvious where a table only lists it.

## Charts

Give each chart one message, and let the title say what to see. Highlight the one bar or line that matters and grey the rest. Label directly instead of using a legend. Skip 3D, pies with eight slices and gridlines doing nothing. Every number gets its source, and every graphic should stand out from the text around it rather than blend in as more grey.

## Structure for use

Headings name the subject ("Pricing", "Risks", "What we recommend"), never the method ("Our Approach, Explained Simply"). Use tables when the reader will compare, prose when they must follow reasoning, and bullets only for items that are truly parallel. Use the reader's own language, never invented internal shorthand, and don't name an internal component to a client when you could say what it does. State a guardrail once, where it matters. A revised document simply says the new thing, with no "as discussed, we've now changed this" and no note about your process.

## Guides and how-tos

Here, explaining is the job and skipping is the failure. Walk every moment as small, literal actions (which tab, which button, what window opens, how to get back), because first-timers stall at exactly the gaps an expert skips. End each step with what's on screen when it worked. A simple labelled drawing beats a paragraph wherever the question is what something looks like. Check the steps against the product as it is today, since interfaces get renamed. In guides for beginners who may not have the thing yet, explain rather than give orders.

## Visual

Check it at phone width first, then printed. Give it a real type scale, one accent colour, generous margins, and page breaks that never strand a heading at the foot of a page. Client-facing work should look like it cost money, because how it looks carries the author's standing as much as what it says.

## The second ditch

- The one-pager compressed until the reasoning is gone, so the reader gets conclusions with nothing to trust them on.
- The executive restyle: bigger headings and a cover page on the same unanswered document.

## The tells

- An executive summary that restates the title.
- A section called "Key Considerations".
- Walls of bullets, and bold on every other phrase.
- Bullets that each open with a bold label and a colon.
- Tables of one-word cells that a sentence would have said better.
- "In conclusion", followed by the introduction again.
- Recommendations hedged until they recommend nothing.
- A diagram at the top built from terms nobody has explained yet.
- Process narration: "We've broken this down simply", "Here's our honest assessment".
