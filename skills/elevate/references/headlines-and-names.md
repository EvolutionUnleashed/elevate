# Headlines, taglines, names and subject lines

Short copy carries the most weight, and it's where describing does the most damage. Everything in writing.md about leaving room applies here, compressed.

## What a naming strategist notices first

Whether the line lands in one second for a busy stranger. Whether it could front a competitor's page unchanged. Whether it needs a sentence of explanation to work, and whether it says what the thing does when it should say why anyone would care.

## Headlines

A headline says one specific thing, at whatever length that takes. On a site's first screen that's usually short: a few words carrying a stance or a feeling. The compressed benefit slogan ("Work, simplified." "Ship faster.") is today's default, so a short line needs a point of view to beat it, and a longer line built on one specific fact can beat any short one.

Don't describe what the thing does in the headline. "The all-in-one platform for managing your projects" fails because every competitor could run it. Describe only what nobody else could say.

The line under the headline makes it plain what this is and who it's for, at the reader's level (what it does for them, not how it works), in new words. It never repeats the headline's key word.

Don't picture the chore you're selling them out of. A line about doing the work well, on a product that does the work for them, puts them back in the chair they want to leave.

Keep to one number per line. Two numbers in different units, or a relative date like "by Friday", make the reader calculate before they feel anything.

If a line needs a beat to decode, say the life it stands for in words people use.

Sell the shift. The top line is the new way things work for the reader, never the task the product performs.

## Taglines

A tagline adds a feeling or a stance the name can't carry. It doesn't explain the product, and a quality claim ("Work you can be proud of") is a tagline anyone could own.

Start from the person's own words if they've given you any. If they hand you a short version of a line, use it as given, and don't grow it back into the sentence it was cut from.

## Names

Product and program names can carry a feeling. They need to land in a second and still mean something.

- A label fails. "Invoice Tracker Pro" says what it is and nothing else.
- A name that needs explaining fails too. "Northstar, because it guides every decision": if it needs the "because", it's word salad, however clever it felt.
- The best names sound like something people already say, or like the moment the thing works.
- Features, sections, menu items, settings and modules are different. They get plain names people can find, such as "Invoices", "Export" and "Team". A clever menu label costs every user a second on every visit.
- One idea from the subject's world is good. A themed world isn't. Calling a program a camp, then the steps "trails", the progress card a "passport" and the files "supplies", turns the whole page into vocabulary the reader has to learn. Read the copy with every themed word removed. If it falls apart, the theme was carrying it.
- Don't let a name dictate the product. A program that runs eleven days because the name has eleven in it is backwards.

## Subject lines

Newsletters and marketing email open a gap: a claim with stakes, an admission, or a turn the reader can't predict the email from. A summary of the argument, or "Issue #12", opens like every other email in the inbox.

Work email says what's inside and what's needed, as in "Q3 report: two figures need your sign-off".

A launch is news to people who asked to hear about it, so lead with the news itself, plainly. "Our new course is here!" opens like every other launch email.

## Buttons and calls to action

Say exactly what happens: "Get the guide", "Book a call", "Start the trial". Keep it plain and proportional. Personality belongs in low-stakes places, never around payment, cancellation or risk, and an arrow bolted onto every button is a tell.

## Finding the line

Run this when the line or the name is the job. Inside a bigger piece, write a handful for the hero and move on.

1. List the angles first: the specific fact, the enemy, the before and after, who it's for, the admission, the objection answered. Write three or four from each, so you get variety rather than twenty versions of one idea.
2. Strike every one that describes, and every one that needs explaining.
3. Read the survivors cold, as a stranger would: the line alone, then in place on the page. Can they say what it means? Would they keep reading?
4. Search for collisions before anyone falls in love with a name. If you can't search, say so.
5. Bring one pick and defend it, with the runner-up and why it lost. A pick someone can reject does more than a menu of ten.
